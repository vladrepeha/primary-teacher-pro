# Assessment, feedback, differentiation, and inclusion

Use for checks for understanding, quizzes, rubrics, learning observation,
formative feedback, tiered tasks, and inclusive supports.

## Build assessment from evidence

For each learning target, define:

- **Observable indicator:** What exactly will a student say, select, solve,
  explain, produce, or perform?
- **Prompt:** A task that elicits the indicator rather than unrelated memory
  or reading load.
- **Success features:** What distinguishes sufficient understanding from
  partial and inaccurate work?
- **Interpretation:** Which wrong responses indicate which likely
  misconception? Do not assume the same wrong answer always has one cause.
- **Next instructional move:** Re-model, add a concrete representation,
  change the example, offer focused practice, or extend.

For quick formative checks, 1–3 revealing questions often beat a long test.
Use low stakes and keep diagnostic questions separate from formal grades.

## Differentiate while preserving the target

| Readiness/support | Adaptation | Preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Needs prerequisite support | Concrete model, steps, partially completed example, reduced extraneous text | Core concept and chance to explain |
| Working at expected level | Independent standard task with explanation or comparison | Same target |
| Ready for challenge | Nonroutine transfer, justify method, find counterexample, create test case | Target, not unrelated busywork |
| Language support | Plain directions, essential glossary, sentence frame, optional bilingual explanation | Intended subject reasoning |
| Accessibility needs | Alternative response mode, spacing, readability, manipulatives, paced steps | Evidence of the same competence |

Do not reduce every supported task to copying answers. Do not infer disability,
neurotype, or "learning style" from a child's performance. Include individual
accommodations when the teacher requests or supplies them.

## Rubrics and descriptors

- Use 2–5 criteria that trace back to the target; no criteria for decorative
  neatness unless presentation itself is a learning outcome.
- Each performance level should describe *observable behavior* and the
  difference between levels, not adjectives alone.
- Provide examples or anchors for ambiguous criteria.
- Use an analytic rubric for feedback by dimension, a holistic rubric for a
  quick overall judgment, or a single-point rubric when the teacher wants
  strengths and next steps around one expected standard.
- Never invent a statistically validated scale or claim research reliability
  for custom descriptors.

Example for **academic engagement** (observation, not a personality label):

| Indicator | Strong evidence | Developing evidence | Needs support |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Initiates task | Begins independently after instructions | Begins after a prompt | Needs guided start |
| Persists through difficulty | Tries an alternative and requests specific help | Tries again after encouragement | Stops without strategy or guided support |

For research tracking: define an observation window, count or frequency,
operational definitions, multiple observations, and a consistent scoring
rule *before* collecting data. Record context; do not overinterpret a single
classroom observation as a stable trait. Obtain appropriate consent and
protect student privacy when applicable.

## Construct good questions

- Use a range of recall, explanation, application, and justified judgment
  only when aligned to the goal.
- For multiple choice, one defensible best answer; plausible alternatives
  based on real misunderstandings; no giveaway through grammar or length.
- For open responses, provide acceptable examples and boundary cases.
- Distinguish numerical accuracy from reasoning quality in mathematics.
- Test each question for unintentional ambiguity and age-level vocabulary.
- If tasks are multilingual, verify translation has not changed the correct
  answer or cognitive demand.

## Feedback pattern

1. Identify a specific successful feature of the student's actual work.
2. Name the most consequential fix or next step, not a list of vague faults.
3. Give a precise action or question the child can attempt immediately.
4. Allow revision or reattempt where feasible.

Do not fabricate evidence from student work that was not provided. Do not
write private assessments of a named child without user-supplied evidence.
